# Response to the reviewers

We thank both reviewers for their careful reading and constructive comments. Below, the reviewers' comments are given in italics, followed by our response. Line numbers refer to the original submission.

## Reviewer 1

### R1 major 1: same data for curation and validation

*The authors claim that the validation is performed using data not used during the curation phase. They show on Figure 1 specific production rates compared to measured values from reference 16 (Sjöberg et al., 2024). However, lines 101-108 state that this same reference provided the fixed glucose uptake rate and was used to verify which reaction to be blocked in certain conditions. It is not clear how this data is used for that and suggests that agreement in predictions might have been biased since the same data is used for curation and validation.*

We agree that Figure 1 is not an independent validation, and we did not intend to present it as one: the Results distinguish "Model evaluation against data used during the curation process" (Figure 1) from "Model validation ... with data unused during the curation phase" (Figure 3). We realise that this distinction was not clear enough. The Sjöberg et al. (2024) batch data were the reference during curation: glucose uptake was fixed at the measured rate, and the measured product rates, the proteome of the same study and the transcriptome of Tai et al. (2005) were used to decide which reactions to constrain. Agreement with these data (Figure 1) therefore shows that the curation achieved its goal, not that the model is validated. We have made this explicit in the Methods, in the caption of Figure 1 and in the Results. Independent evaluation is provided by data that were not used during curation: the anaerobic 13C flux distributions of four studies (Figure 3C-D), the chemostat growth rates (Figure 3A-B) and, new in this revision, the phenotypes of deletion and engineered strains (see our reply to Reviewer 2, comment 1). We note that in this revision we also used the 13C flux data to evaluate three constraints that the reviewer questioned (glycine cleavage, glutamate synthase and Gcy1), and we state this where those constraints are discussed.

### R1 major 2: state of the old anaerobic function

*The state of the old version of the function is not explicitly mentioned, as which genes or reaction are blocked are not stated in the text. One could read the old version of the code, sure, but their absence in the current manuscript that claims to correct them leads to confusion of what is then done here. For instance, it is unclear if Mdh2 is blocked already or if newly introduced. This confuses the argument in lines 142-147, where the authors state that glycerol was blocked in the previous version of the model without proper justification to do so. However, Mdh2 (line 154), Gcy1 (line 238) and Gcv2 (line 286) are introduced precisely because they block glycerol. The paper cannot criticize a previous decision and still use it without making the rationale clear.*

We agree that the manuscript should state what the previous anaerobic function did. The new Supplementary Table SX lists every constraint of the previous function and of the updated constraints, with the status of each (kept, modified, removed or new), where it is applied (anaerobic function or model file), the supporting evidence, and the predicted glycerol production when only that constraint is undone. Our criticism of the previous function was not that it restricted these reactions, but that the restrictions were chosen to reproduce the glycerol yield without stating the evidence. We re-examined each of them. The reductive direction of Mdh2 was already blocked; we now block Mdh2 completely, as it is inactivated by glucose (Hung et al. 2004) and 260-fold lower in the anaerobic proteome (Table 1); in the final model this block no longer affects the predictions. The block of Gcy1 was replaced by an irreversible reaction in the thermodynamically favoured direction (reply to comment 6). The block of glutamate synthase (Glt1) is kept: GOGAT is an ancillary route of ammonium assimilation when ammonium is in excess (Valenzuela et al. 1998), and in anaerobic batch cultures on glucose and ammonium the activity of the NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase is 50-100 times higher than that of the other enzymes of ammonium assimilation (Nissen et al. 2000), whereas a model without this constraint assimilates all ammonium through the NADH-dependent GOGAT. We state explicitly that the enzyme is expressed under these conditions (Table 1), that the block represents this regulation rather than the absence of the enzyme, and that without it the predicted glycerol production drops from 4.35 to 2.30 mmol/gDW/h. The block of the glycine cleavage system is new and based on its repression by nitrogen catabolite repression and its induction by glycine (Piper et al. 2002; Nagarajan and Storms 1997); we now also cite 13C studies that indicate glycine cleavage activity in anaerobic cultures (Maaheimo et al. 2001; Jouhten et al. 2008) and show the effect of the block (glycerol 3.41 without it). For glutamate synthase, glycine cleavage and Gcy1, removing the constraint also worsens the agreement with the independent 13C flux data (Supplementary Table SY). The restriction of Mdh1 to the oxidative direction was removed, as we found no evidence for it.

### R1 major 3: which change improves the predictions

*The curation introduces many changes to the yeast model, but prediction performance is only evaluated for the finished version. It is not clear what exactly leads to better predictions: whether it was the anaerobic constraints, charge and elemental balance, changes in the biomass equation, or curation of mitochondrial and nitrogen metabolism. Further, the changes are not fully described, for instance lines*
*208-218 mentions four different changes to the biomass equation but none of them are explained. The addition of NADH to the biomass introduces new inconsistencies and is also not addressed: this leads to cytosolic redox being coupled to growth which could directly influences glycerol production.*

We agree that the manuscript did not show which changes caused the improvement. We now separate the contributions in three ways (new Supplementary Tables SZ1-SZ5, Supplementary Figures S_b and S_c). First, the 2×2 combination of model version (Yeast9.0.2 or 9.1.2) and anaerobic constraints (original or updated). The updated constraints alone give no glycerol on Yeast9.0.2 (0.00 mmol/gDW/h; measured 4.5 ± 0.4); the original constraints give 3.96 on Yeast9.0.2 and 5.29 on Yeast9.1.2 (with the thiamine uptake that the curated model needs without oxygen). Only the combination of Yeast9.1.2 and the updated constraints gives glycerol within the measurement error (4.35), the best growth predictions (R² 0.90, against 0.79, 0.85 and 0.67 for the other three combinations) and the lowest error against the 13C fluxes (mean relative error 30%, against 40%, 53% and 47%). Second, we undid each of the eight changes of the updated anaerobic constraints in turn, and each pair of them. The improvement in growth predictions comes from using the general growth-associated maintenance instead of the condition-specific values (anaerobic C-limited chemostats: relative error 5.7% instead of 28%; growth R² 0.90 instead of 0.78). Glycerol production changes by more than 0.1 mmol/gDW/h only for these values (+1.0), for the block of the cytosolic NADP-isocitrate dehydrogenase Idp2 (-0.37) and for the restriction of Mdh1 to the oxidative direction (+0.13); the other five changes (tetraenol uptake, Mdh2, amino-acid ratio, cofactor pseudoreaction and FADH2 in the biomass) change it by less than 0.02. Third, we replayed the curation from Yeast9.0.2 to 9.1.0, with the later changes of Yeast9.1.2, in 20 parts and grouped them in the four categories named by the reviewer (charge and element balance; biomass equation; mitochondrial and nitrogen metabolism; reaction directions and cofactors) and a fifth for the changes after 9.1.0. We left out each part, each category and each pair of parts from Yeast9.1.2, and added each category alone to Yeast9.0.2 (Supplementary Tables SZ3 and SZ4, Supplementary Figures S_b and S_c). Glycerol production depends on the direction of Gcy1 (no glycerol without it), the blocks of glutamate synthase (2.30), glycine cleavage (3.41) and carnitine acetyltransferase (3.81), the irreversibility of two ester-forming reactions (3.72 without the esterification change) and the allowed export of glycine, without which the model hardly grows. The irreversibility of the proton symporters and of the ester-forming reaction are the main determinants of the agreement with the 13C fluxes (error 0.77 and 0.51 without them, against 0.30). The charge and element balance and the biomass equation, taken as categories, leave glycerol (4.32 and 4.35) and growth (R² 0.90) unchanged, and the changes made after Yeast9.1.0 (thiamine, fumarate reductases, TYR1 cofactor, acetate transport and ribonucleotide reductase) do not change any of the predictions. The two categories that matter do not act independently. Leaving out either the mitochondrial and nitrogen category or the reaction-direction category removes the glycerol, and neither gives glycerol when added alone to Yeast9.0.2; they also act in opposite directions on the growth predictions (the directions category improves the anaerobic C-limited chemostats, the mitochondrial and nitrogen category worsens them). Of the 136 pairs of curation parts that can be compared, 9 interact by more than 0.5 mmol/gDW/h in glycerol, mostly through the glycine export. We now describe each of these biomass changes explicitly. Regarding the reducing equivalents in the biomass reaction: the model uses NADPH, not NADH as erroneously stated, so the term does not couple cytosolic NAD(H) to growth. On re-examination, the biomass of Yeast9.0.2 already had a degree of reduction of 4.20 per C-mol, and the added NADPH raised it to 4.37; leaving it out changes the predicted glycerol production by +0.4% (4.37 instead of 4.35) and the error against the 13C fluxes from 0.31 to 0.28.

### R1 major 4: GAM, NGAM and protein content are fitted

*GAM, NGAM and protein content are fitted parameters, but the manuscript presents them as fixed inputs in lines 103-104. The text points to a reference, but it's impossible to know how the authors found these values from the text alone.*

We agree that the text was unclear. GAM (55.3 mmol ATP/gDW) and NGAM (0.7 mmol ATP/gDW/h) were not fitted in this study: they are the values of yeast-GEM, where GAM was fitted (release 8.3.1) to aerobic glucose-limited chemostat data (van Hoek et al. 1998) by minimising the relative error in glucose and oxygen uptake and CO2 production at fixed growth rates. The previous anaerobic function replaced these by anaerobic-specific values (GAM 30.49, NGAM 0; Nissen et al. 1997); the updated constraints use the same values for all conditions, which by itself improves the anaerobic growth predictions (relative error in anaerobic C-limited chemostats 5.7% instead of 28%). The protein content (0.46 g/gDW) was not fitted either: it results from rescaling the protein fraction so that the biomass components sum to 1 g/gDW. For nitrogen-limited conditions we set it to 0.28 g/gDW, as measured in ammonium-limited chemostat cultures of CEN.PK113-7D (Boer et al. 2005); the manuscript erroneously stated 0.29. We have rewritten the Methods accordingly.

### R1 major 5: pFBA degeneracy

*The optima for pFBA are often degenerate and every claim on better predictive performance relies on a single solution from pFBA. Without flux variability or sampling, it is not possible to claim whether curation was sufficient to improve performance since it could well be explained by how the solver chooses an optimum.*

We thank the reviewer for this important point. We have sampled the space of optimal solutions uniformly (Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo; Kook et al. 2022) for each model and condition, with four independent chains (rank-normalised R̂ ≤ 1.004 for every reaction), and repeated the analysis allowing growth down to 99% of the optimum (new Methods section, Supplementary Figure S_a). The results do not depend on the bound used for internal fluxes. The predictions shown in Figure 1 (growth, glycerol, ethanol, CO2) are fully determined at the optimum: all optimal solutions give the pFBA values. The fluxes compared with the 13C data (Figure 3C-D) are nearly determined for Yeast9.1.2 (median width of the 95% interval below 1e-4 % of the glucose uptake; 8 of the 83 compared fluxes have an interval wider than 1%, at most 3.2%), but not for Yeast9.0.2, where even the glycolytic fluxes have intervals of about 90% of the glucose uptake. Scoring all optimal solutions against the 13C data (continuous ranked probability score) gives the same ranking of the model versions as the pFBA solution, in each of the four chains; per data set, Yeast9.1.2 with the updated constraints scores best for two of the seven data sets (Celton et al. 2012; Wasylenko and Stephanopoulos 2014), Yeast9.1.2 with the original constraints for Jouhten et al. (2008) and Yeast9.0.2 with the original constraints for the four data sets of Nissen et al.. When growth is allowed to drop to 99% of the optimum, the order of the four models on the two 13C data sets sampled in this analysis (Jouhten et al. 2008; Wasylenko and Stephanopoulos 2014) does not change (mean CRPS 2.23, 2.54, 4.89 and 9.07), and the two Yeast9.1.2 variants remain distinguishable. Some fluxes discussed in the text are not determined by the optimum, and we now say so: the split between Idp1 and Idh1/2, and the flux through the plasma membrane H+-ATPase, of which the ratio to ammonium uptake (Figure 2) is a choice of pFBA rather than a requirement of the model (see also our reply to Reviewer 2, comment 3).

### R1 major 6: contradictory Gcy1 paragraph

*Lines 242-247: this paragraph makes contradicting claims. It says that the reaction should be blocked based on thermodynamics, supporting the previous version of the yeast model for anaerobiosis, then immediately say that it must be active for microaerobic glycerol assimilation. It is not clear what the authors actually change.*

We agree that the paragraph was contradictory, and it described the change incorrectly. In Yeast9.1.2 Gcy1 is not blocked: the reaction is written as the reduction of dihydroxyacetone with NADPH and is irreversible. Run in the oxidative direction, Gcy1 together with dihydroxyacetone kinase, glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and glycerol-3-phosphatase converts cytosolic NADH into NADPH at the cost of one ATP; the previous text wrongly stated that this happens without energy input. The constraint is based on thermodynamics: the reaction energy of glycerol oxidation is ΔrG'm = +22.6 ± 1.8 kJ/mol (eQuilibrator), and with the measured cytosolic NADPH/NADP+ ratio of 16-22 (Zhang et al. 2015) the oxidative direction requires the intracellular dihydroxyacetone concentration to be below 1/70,000 to 1/420,000 of the glycerol concentration. With dihydroxyacetone at 5 µM, the lower Km of the dihydroxyacetone kinases (Molin et al. 2003), this requires 0.35 to 2.1 M intracellular glycerol, a range reached during osmotic stress, where Gcy1 indeed contributes to glycerol dissimilation (Norbeck and Blomberg 1997). Intracellular glycerol has not been measured for anaerobic glucose cultures, so we assume that the oxidative direction is inactive in anaerobic cultures on glucose; for conditions of high intracellular glycerol the constraint would have to be relaxed, which we now state. Without it, the model would predict no glycerol production at all, and the agreement with the 13C flux data would worsen (Supplementary Table SY).

### R1 major 7: reproducibility and backward compatibility

*Reproducibility and backwards compatibility are not guaranteed in what is currently presented. A Python version of the code is mentioned, but there is no statement if it faithfully reproduces the MATLAB version or not. Further, the function is renamed, meaning that any script using the old implementation will now produce different results after updating it or not work at all. That is the opposite of backward compatibility, and the consequences should be properly addressed.*

We agree. The anaerobic constraints are now defined once, in a data file (data/conditions/anaerobic.yml), which is applied by applyEnvironment(model,'anaerobic') in MATLAB and by yeastgem.conditions.apply(model, 'anaerobic') in Python. Neither depends on another toolbox. The repository contains a check (code/python/tests/reference) that applies every environment in both languages and confirms identical bounds and stoichiometry; it does for all five environments (anaerobic, minimal medium, glycine as nitrogen source, nitrogen limitation, carnitine). Regarding backward compatibility: the name anaerobicModel again returns the constraints it had before Yeast9.1.0, with a warning that it is deprecated, so scripts written for earlier releases reproduce their results; anaerobicModelOld remains available. The updated constraints are only applied through the new name. We note that in Yeast9.1.0 itself the name anaerobicModel returned the updated constraints; this is now stated in the release notes. (yeast-GEM pull requests #421 and #422.)

## Reviewer 2

### R2 major 1: validation on deletion strains

*Currently model validation relies on the prediction of growth rates and measured intracellular fluxes of WT yeasts, whereas it would be very beneficial to showcase the improved predictive power on the metabolic states of deletion strains. Not an awful lot of experimental datasets are available, but there have been a couple of mutants in central metabolism characterized in anaerobic chemostats at TU Delft (cf. Bakker 2000, Boles 1998) - these two have been also used in another genome-scale modeling study on yeast anaerobic growth (Grigaitis 2023).*

We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We have added an evaluation against deletion strains characterised under anaerobic conditions (new Figure/Table): adh3 (Bakker et al. 2000), mae1 (Boles et al. 1998), gpd1, gpd2 and gpd1 gpd2 (Ansell et al. 1997; Nissen et al. 2000), ald6 (Papapetridis et al. 2016), frd1, osm1 and frd1 osm1 (Arikawa et al. 1998; Camarasa et al. 2007), and gdh1 (Nissen et al. 2000), as well as engineered strains with altered redox or energy metabolism. When the measured glucose uptake rate is imposed, Yeast9.1.2 with the updated constraints predicts the growth rate and glycerol production of the wild type and the adh3 mutant of Bakker et al. (2000) within the experimental error; however, this agreement results from the lower glucose uptake of the mutant, as the model itself predicts no effect of the adh3 deletion at equal uptake, also when gpd2 is deleted together with adh3 as in Grigaitis et al. (2023). Grigaitis et al. predicted the lower growth rate of the adh3 mutant with a proteome-constrained model, in which the maximal growth rate is limited by enzyme capacity; yeast-GEM has no such constraint. As in their study, the mae1 deletion has a small effect in our simulations (biomass yield -2.4%), in agreement with Boles et al. (1998). Across the deletion strains with anaerobic growth data (Supplementary Table S11, Supplementary Figure S_d), the predicted growth class (normal, impaired or no growth) agrees with the observation for 10 of 18 combinations of strain and study. The model correctly predicts that the fumarate reductase double mutant cannot grow anaerobically and that the single mutants of frd1, kgd1, sdh1 and ald4, ald5 and ald6 grow normally, and it predicts the loss of glycerol production of the gpd1 gpd2 mutant (yield 0.00 against 0.09 of the wild type). It predicts biomass yields of reference and engineered strains within 5-10%, and the ethanol yield of all deletion strains within 1%. It fails to predict the anaerobic growth defect of gpd2 and osm1 and of the ald6 ald4 ald5 triple mutant, the lower glycerol yields of the single gpd1 and gpd2 mutants (0.6-1.0 of the wild type, against 1.0 predicted) and the inviability of gpd1 gpd2 (predicted growth 11% below the wild type). In the gpd1 gpd2 mutant the model reoxidises NADH through an overflow of the threonine pathway with export of propanol (2.7 mmol/gDW/h), and through glycine and succinate formation when propanol export is closed. Each step is a reaction that exists in yeast, so we have not changed the model for this strain; and the frd1 single mutant was predicted inviable due to an incorrect gene association, which we corrected (yeast-GEM pull request #423; Yeast9.1.2 predicts that the frd1 and osm1 single mutants grow and that the double mutant does not). We discuss these disagreements as limitations of a stoichiometric model, which does not represent regulation of the enzymes or their capacity; we have not tested this explanation.

### R2 major 2: what Figure 3C-D compares

*Figure 3, panels C-D: I cannot immediately judge what these plots show based on the figure caption, as it is too vague. Is it the sum flux through the system? If so, how did the Authors treat the predicted vs. experimentally measured flux - were only measured fluxes included in the comparison in both datasets? (probably the fluxes that were not/cannot be measured were all small values, but still relevant). Which fluxes (and of which magnitude) had highest deviations from the experimental values?*

We have rewritten the caption and added a Supplementary Table with all compared fluxes. Panels C-D compare, for each of seven data sets, every measured flux that has a corresponding reaction in the model with the flux predicted at the measured glucose uptake rate, both expressed as percentage of the glucose uptake (83 fluxes for Yeast9.1.2; 80 for Yeast9.0.2, which lacks the mitochondrial oxaloacetate transporter Oac1; the seven glucose uptake rates are not counted). Fluxes that were not measured are not included. Because glucose uptake is imposed, the seven glucose uptake values agree by construction; we now report the statistics without them. The large fluxes of glycolysis and fermentation (above 50% of the glucose uptake) are predicted with a mean relative error of 3.6%; the largest absolute deviations are an over-prediction of CO2 production by 12% of the glucose uptake in one study (Nissen et al., condition 4) and of ethanol production by 10% in another (Jouhten et al. 2008). Small fluxes (below 5% of glucose uptake) have a mean relative error of 55%; the largest relative deviations are for acetate formation and acetyl-CoA synthetase, which the model does not predict, NAD-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase and threonine aldolase.

### R2 major 3: proton diffusion and the plasma-membrane ATPase

*In some yeast-GEM iterations there were reactions which would allow diffusion of protons across membranes (e.g. H+[e] <=> H+[c] and H+[c] <=> H+[m]), which could cause artifacts in simulations, especially in anaerobic conditions. Did you check whether these reactions (if present in yeast9) carry flux in any of the simulations? I do see in Fig 2 that the plasma membrane ATPase carries flux in the updated model simulations, though. Authors attribute to modifications in ammonia import in the text - I was wondering whether the model simulations point into glycolytic flux also acidifying the cells, setting demand for ATP-costly transport of extra protons outside of the cell.*

We checked these reactions. Yeast9 contains reactions for proton diffusion over the plasma membrane and into the mitochondria; at the optimum they carry at most 0.0005 mmol/gDW/h in Yeast9.1.2 (0.0015 in Yeast9.0.2) by flux variability analysis. When glucose uptake and growth are each allowed to deviate by 1% from the optimum, the analysis permits up to 0.9 mmol/gDW/h in Yeast9.1.2 as an extreme, against 2.3 mmol/gDW/h through the H+-ATPase. Regarding the origin of the proton load: in the model, glycolysis and alcoholic fermentation are proton-neutral (the protons consumed when glycolysis forms ATP are released when the ATP is used). The flux through the H+-ATPase in the pFBA solution (2.32 mmol/gDW/h; Figure 2) is close to the uptake of ammonium (2.49 mmol/gDW/h), the proton of which is released into the cytosol when the nitrogen is assimilated. However, flux variability analysis shows that the ATPase flux itself is not determined: the same protons can be exported by the Na+/H+ antiporter running in reverse coupled to Na+ export by Ena ATPases, at the same ATP cost. We now state this in the text and in the caption of Figure 2.
