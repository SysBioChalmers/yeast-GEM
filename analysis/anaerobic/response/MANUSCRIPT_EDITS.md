## Methods

### l.89-101 (software, functions): R1 major 7, R1 minor 2
All simulations were performed using parsimonious flux balance analysis (pFBA) (13,14) through the RAVEN toolbox 2.11.1 (15) in MATLAB (2026a), with the solver Gurobi Optimizer (13.0.2). The starting point for the curation was the consensus Yeast9.0.2 model and the anaerobic constraints previously distributed with yeast-GEM (function anaerobicModel), here referred to as the original anaerobic constraints; the curated constraints are referred to as the updated anaerobic constraints. The original function is kept as anaerobicModelOld, and anaerobicModel returns the original constraints with a warning that it is deprecated, to preserve backward compatibility. The updated constraints are defined in a data file (data/conditions/anaerobic.yml) that is applied by applyEnvironment(model,'anaerobic') in MATLAB and by yeastgem.conditions.apply(model, 'anaerobic') in Python; neither requires another toolbox. A test in the repository confirms that both give identical bounds and stoichiometry for every condition file. Other condition files make the condition-specific reactions available when required: glycine as nitrogen source (glycine cleavage system), nitrogen limitation (glutamate synthase and glycine cleavage system) and carnitine in the medium (cytosolic carnitine O-acetyltransferase). All are available from https://github.com/SysBioChalmers/yeast-GEM.

### l.103-116 (model curation): R1 majors 1 and 4
[keep first three sentences] The model was curated to match the metabolic profile of anaerobic batch cultures in defined synthetic medium with glucose as the carbon source and ammonium in excess (Sjöberg et al. 2024 (16)): glucose uptake was fixed at the measured 23 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹, and the predicted biomass, ethanol, glycerol and CO2 production were compared with the measured rates at each iteration. Proteomics data from the same study and transcriptomics data from chemostat cultures (Tai et al. (17)) were used to decide whether reactions should be disabled under these conditions. These data were therefore used during curation; the data used for validation (below) were not. Growth-associated (GAM, 55.3 mmol ATP·gDW⁻¹) and non-growth-associated maintenance (NGAM, 0.7 mmol ATP·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹) are the yeast-GEM values and were not fitted in this study: GAM was fitted in yeast-GEM release 8.3.1 to aerobic glucose-limited chemostat data (van Hoek et al. 1998) by minimising the relative error in glucose and oxygen uptake and CO2 production at the measured growth rates. The same values are used for all conditions, whereas the original anaerobic constraints used anaerobic-specific values (GAM 30.49, NGAM 0; Nissen et al. 1997). The protein content (0.46 g·gDW⁻¹) is not a fitted parameter either: it results from scaling the protein fraction so that the biomass components sum to 1 g·gDW⁻¹.

### l.117-126 (data for validation): R1 major 1, R2 major 1
Data for model validation. None of the following data were used during curation. [keep growth benchmark and fluxomics sentences] Flux ratios were compared for every measured flux with a corresponding model reaction (83 fluxes over seven data sets for Yeast9.1.2; 80 for Yeast9.0.2, which lacks the mitochondrial oxaloacetate/sulphate antiporter Oac1; the glucose uptake rates are not counted), as absolute values relative to the glucose uptake rate; unmeasured fluxes were not compared (Supplementary Table S10). For the growth benchmarks, the protein content was 0.46 g·gDW⁻¹ under carbon limitation and 0.28 g·gDW⁻¹ under nitrogen limitation, as measured in ammonium-limited chemostat cultures (Boer et al. 2005), with RNA assumed to decrease by the same proportion (0.0329 g·gDW⁻¹). [l.352: "0.29" → "0.28" as well.] Deletion and engineered strains characterised under anaerobic conditions (Supplementary Table S11) were simulated with the measured glucose uptake rate (batch) or dilution rate (chemostat), with the deleted genes removed through the gene-reaction rules.

### New: sampling: R1 major 5
Flux sampling. To assess whether conclusions depend on the particular pFBA solution, the space of optimal solutions was sampled uniformly for each model, set of constraints and condition. Growth (or, where growth was fixed, glucose uptake) was constrained to at least 99.999% of its optimum (optimal face), or to at least 99% in a sensitivity analysis. Internal (non-exchange) reaction bounds were limited to ±200 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹, and samples were drawn with constrained Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (PolytopeSamplerMatlab; Kook et al. 2022), four independent chains (different seeds) of about 1000 samples each. Thermodynamically infeasible cycles were removed from every sample by cycle-free projection (CycleFreeFlux); the largest projected internal flux was 43 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹, below the bound of 200. Convergence was assessed over the four chains with the rank-normalised split R̂ and the bulk and tail effective sample size (Vehtari et al. 2021): R̂ ≤ 1.004 and bulk/tail ESS ≥ 1700 for every reaction that varied, in every model and condition. Repeating the sampling with bounds of ±100 and ±400 gave the same quantiles for the internal fluxes. Flux variability analysis was performed for comparison. Predictions were compared with 13C flux data with the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), computed over all samples.

### l.140-149 (vitamins, sterols): R1 major 2
For the anaerobic constraints, the first step was to open the exchange reactions for the vitamins nicotinate and (R)-pantothenate, whose synthesis requires molecular oxygen (22,23) and which are added to defined synthetic media irrespective of oxygen availability. Sterols must also be provided during strictly anaerobic growth, and the original function allowed uptake of ergosterol, several sterol intermediates, oleate and palmitoleate. Among the sterol intermediates, ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraen-3β-ol was used by the model as a substrate for ergosterol formation, which by the Erg4-catalysed reduction consumes NADPH; as this intermediate is not a medium component, its uptake was removed. Note that the model requires unsaturated fatty acids from the medium under anaerobic conditions, because the fatty-acid composition of its biomass is fixed; S. cerevisiae can grow anaerobically without them by changing its fatty-acid composition (Dekker et al. 2019).

### l.151-153: R1 major 2

We found it justified to block the cytosolic malate dehydrogenase (Mdh2) entirely in the absence of oxygen; the original function had already blocked its reductive direction. ### l.196-201 (FADH2): R1 major 3, decision 2
Option A (0.019): In addition, a FADH2 to FAD conversion was added to the biomass reaction to account for disulphide bond formation by Ero1 in the endoplasmic reticulum, which reduces FAD; under anaerobic conditions the FAD of Ero1 is reoxidised by fumarate reductase, mainly the ER form of Osm1 (Kim et al. 2018). As the model has no ER pool of FAD, the conversion is placed in the cytosol, where both fumarate reductases can reoxidise it. We set it to 0.019 mmol·gDW⁻¹, the upper limit given by the cysteine content of the biomass protein (every cysteine in a disulphide); its value does not affect the predicted product rates, but its presence makes fumarate reductase essential under anaerobic conditions, as observed for the frd1 osm1 double mutant (Arikawa et al. 1998; Camarasa et al. 2007).
Option B (0.08): same text up to "can reoxidise it", then: "Its coefficient (0.08 mmol·gDW⁻¹) was chosen to reproduce the succinate production of anaerobic chemostat cultures (Nissen et al. 1997; Celton et al. 2012); it exceeds the 0.019 mmol·gDW⁻¹ that disulphide formation alone could account for (every cysteine of biomass protein in a disulphide), which implies further FAD-dependent oxidations, for instance by Erv1-Mia40 in the mitochondrial intermembrane space. ### l.211-221 (charge balance, ATPase): R2 major 3, R1 major 5
[keep l.211-217] ..., the predicted export of protons increased; the flux through the plasma membrane H+-ATPase in the pFBA solution (2.32 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹) is close to the uptake of ammonium (2.49 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹). This is expected, as at the typical extracellular pH values for S. cerevisiae (pH < 6) (39) ammonia is fully ionized, and the proton of ammonium is released when the nitrogen is assimilated (40); glycolysis and alcoholic fermentation themselves are proton-neutral. In the pFBA solution the protons are exported by the plasma membrane H+-ATPase (Figure 2), but flux variability analysis shows that the same export can be achieved by the Na+/H+ antiporter running in reverse coupled to Na+ export by the Ena ATPases, at the same ATP cost; the flux through the H+-ATPase is therefore not determined by the model. Reactions for passive proton diffusion over the plasma and mitochondrial membranes are present in Yeast9 but carry at most 0.0005 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹ in any optimal solution.

### l.250-265 (Gcy1): R1 major 6
Production of NADPH is also associated with an ATP cost. [keep l.250-252] During this process we found cycles that converted NADH into NADPH. The most important of these involved Gcy1, which was defined in the oxidative direction (glycerol + NADP+ → dihydroxyacetone + NADPH): together with dihydroxyacetone kinase, glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and glycerol-3-phosphatase it converts cytosolic NADH into NADPH at the cost of one ATP, which allowed the model to avoid glycerol formation. The original anaerobic function blocked this reaction. We instead wrote it in the reductive direction and made it irreversible, based on thermodynamics: the reaction energy of glycerol oxidation is ΔrG'm = +22.6 ± 1.8 kJ·mol⁻¹ (eQuilibrator; 25), and with a cytosolic NADPH/NADP+ ratio of 16-22 (Zhang et al. 2015) the oxidative direction requires the intracellular dihydroxyacetone concentration to be below 1/70,000 to 1/420,000 of the glycerol concentration. With dihydroxyacetone at 5 µM, the lower Km of the dihydroxyacetone kinases (Molin et al. 2003), this requires 0.35 to 2.1 M intracellular glycerol. Intracellular glycerol has not been measured for anaerobic glucose cultures, so we assume that the oxidative direction is inactive in these cultures. Molar glycerol concentrations occur during osmotic stress, where Gcy1 contributes to glycerol dissimilation (Norbeck and Blomberg 1997); for such conditions, and for glycerol assimilation under microaerobic conditions (46), the reaction would have to be made reversible. Without this constraint the model predicts no glycerol formation, and the agreement with the 13C flux data worsens (Supplementary Table SY). Gcy1 is expressed under anaerobic conditions (Table 1).

### l.280-284 (carnitine): R1 major 2
Finally, the carnitine-dependent transfer of acetyl units into the mitochondria was eliminated, as no carnitine is supplied in the medium and S. cerevisiae lacks the enzymes for carnitine synthesis (51). Because carnitine only cycles as a carrier in the model, the shuttle would otherwise run without any carnitine supply; we therefore blocked the cytosolic carnitine O-acetyltransferase (r_0252) and provide a condition file that reopens it, together with carnitine uptake, for media that contain carnitine.

### l.297-305 (Glt1): R1 major 2, decision 1
In nitrogen excess, the main route of ammonia assimilation is the NADPH-dependent Gdh1 (53), and the GS-GOGAT route is an ancillary pathway (Valenzuela et al. 1998); in anaerobic batch cultures on glucose and ammonium the activity of Gdh1 is 50-100 times higher than that of the other enzymes of ammonium assimilation (Nissen et al. 2000). Without further constraints, however, the model assimilated all ammonium through Gln1-Glt1, which oxidises NADH instead of NADPH and thereby replaces glycerol formation as an NADH sink. Glt1 is expressed under these conditions (Table 1), and its capacity of about 0.8 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹ (Nissen et al. 2000) would allow part of the ammonium to be assimilated through it. We assume that Gdh1 carries the ammonium assimilation and that Glt1 carries no flux in ammonium excess, which is not directly measured in anaerobic cultures. We blocked the glutamate synthase reaction r_0472 in the default model, which also applies to aerobic simulations without changing their predictions, and provide a condition file that reopens it for nitrogen limitation. Without this block, the predicted glycerol production decreases from 4.35 to 2.30 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹ and the agreement with the 13C flux data worsens (Supplementary Table SY). A gdh1 deletion is predicted to grow through Gdh3, consistent with the growth of gdh1 glt1 strains on ammonium (Avendaño et al. 1997); a gdh1 gdh3 double deletion is predicted not to grow, which to our knowledge has not been tested.

### l.307-319 (GCV): R1 major 2
Another factor limiting the production of glycerol was the glycine cleavage system. Glycine cleavage is under nitrogen catabolite repression (Piper et al. 2002) and induced by glycine (Nagarajan and Storms 1997). 13C labelling of anaerobic batch cultures on glucose and ammonium gives a lower bound of 19% of the cytosolic glycine originating from mitochondrial glycine (Maaheimo et al. 2001), whereas Jouhten et al. (2008) report 0 to 6%; both concern the glycine-forming direction, and the forward cleavage has not been measured anaerobically, while aerobic studies find zero or very low flux. Gcv2 is detected in the anaerobic proteome (Table 1). Without constraints, the model used glycine cleavage in a route from threonine through glycine to serine that produces mitochondrial NADH and replaces part of the glycerol formation (glycerol 3.41 instead of 4.35 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹), and the agreement with the 13C data worsened (Supplementary Table SY). We therefore assume that glycine cleavage carries no flux and blocked the reactions r_0501, r_0507 and r_0509 in the default model, which does not change aerobic predictions, and provide condition files that reopen them when glycine is the nitrogen source or under nitrogen limitation. With glycine cleavage blocked, the model needs to export a small amount of glycine (0.13% of the glucose uptake), formed as a by-product of C1 metabolism; such low-level excretion of amino acids by yeast has been reported (Paczia et al. 2012).

### New Results paragraph: attribution: R1 major 3
To identify which changes improved the predictions, we combined the two model versions with the two sets of anaerobic constraints, undid each of the eight changes of the updated anaerobic constraints and each pair of them, and replayed the curation from Yeast9.0.2 to 9.1.0, with the later changes of Yeast9.1.2, in 20 parts grouped in five categories (Supplementary Tables SZ1-SZ5, Supplementary Figures S_b and S_c). The updated anaerobic constraints alone did not lead to glycerol formation in Yeast9.0.2, and the original constraints gave 3.96 on Yeast9.0.2 and 5.29 on Yeast9.1.2 (measured 4.5 ± 0.4 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹); only the combination of Yeast9.1.2 and the updated constraints predicted glycerol within the measurement error (4.35), the best growth predictions (R² 0.90, against 0.79, 0.85 and 0.67) and the lowest error against the 13C fluxes (30%, against 40%, 53% and 47%). The improvement of the growth predictions came mainly from using the same maintenance energy for all conditions (relative error in anaerobic carbon-limited chemostats 5.7% instead of 28% with the anaerobic-specific values of the original constraints). Of the constraints, only this value (+1.0), the block of Idp2 (-0.37) and the restriction of Mdh1 (+0.13) changed glycerol by more than 0.1 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹. Of the curation parts, the predicted glycerol production depended on the direction of Gcy1 (no glycerol without it), the blocks of glutamate synthase (2.30), glycine cleavage (3.41) and carnitine acetyltransferase (3.81), the irreversibility of two ester-forming reactions (3.72) and the export of glycine; the irreversibility of the proton symporters and of the ester-forming reaction were the main determinants of the agreement with the 13C fluxes (error 0.77 and 0.51 without them, against 0.30). Changes to charge and element balance and to the biomass equation, and the changes after Yeast9.1.0 (thiamine, fumarate reductases, TYR1 cofactor, acetate transport, ribonucleotide reductase), did not change the predictions. The mitochondrial and nitrogen metabolism category and the reaction-direction category did not act independently: leaving out either removed the glycerol, neither produced it when added alone to Yeast9.0.2, and they affected the growth predictions in opposite directions. Of 136 pairs of curation parts, 9 interacted by more than 0.5 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹, mostly through the glycine export.

### New Results paragraph: flux sampling: R1 major 5
Alternative optimal solutions. pFBA returns one of possibly many flux distributions with the same optimal growth, so we sampled all optimal solutions of each model and condition (Methods). For Yeast9.1.2 with the updated anaerobic constraints, growth and the production of glycerol, ethanol and CO2 under the conditions of Figure 1 had the same value in every optimal solution, as did the uptake of ammonium (2.49 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹) and its assimilation by NADP-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase (2.18 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹). Not determined were the split of isocitrate oxidation between Idp1 and Idh1/2 and the flux through the plasma membrane H+-ATPase (0-2.33 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹), for which Figure 2 shows one of several equivalent solutions. The fluxes compared with the 13C data (Figure 3C-D) were nearly determined in Yeast9.1.2 (median width of the 95% interval below 10⁻⁴ % of the glucose uptake; 8 of 83 intervals wider than 1%, at most 3.2%), whereas Yeast9.0.2 left even the glycolytic flux undetermined (aldolase 2.2-93% of the glucose uptake). Scoring all optimal solutions against the 13C data with the continuous ranked probability score (lower is better) gave the same ranking as the pFBA comparison: Yeast9.1.2 with the updated constraints 2.51, with the original constraints 2.68, Yeast9.0.2 with the original constraints 3.75 and with the updated constraints 9.45, identical in each of four independent chains (Supplementary Table S12). Per data set, the updated constraints scored best for two of the three 13C studies (Celton et al. 2012; Wasylenko and Stephanopoulos 2014), Yeast9.1.2 with the original constraints for the third (Jouhten et al. 2008), and Yeast9.0.2 with the original constraints for the four dilution rates of Nissen et al. (1997), for which the updated model over-predicts ethanol and CO2 production by 5-12% of the glucose uptake. When growth was allowed to drop to 99% of the optimum, the fluxes that were fixed at the optimum changed by about 1% (glycerol 4.32-4.34 instead of 4.35 mmol·gDW⁻¹·h⁻¹), the undetermined ones kept their range, and the order of the four models on the two 13C data sets sampled in this analysis (Jouhten et al. 2008; Wasylenko and Stephanopoulos 2014) did not change (mean CRPS 2.23, 2.54, 4.89 and 9.07).

### New Results section: deletion strains: R2 major 1
Deletion and engineered strains. We compared the predictions with the anaerobic phenotypes of deletion strains from the literature (Supplementary Table S11, Supplementary Figure S_d). The growth class (normal, impaired, none) was predicted correctly for 10 of 18 combinations of strain and study. Yeast9.1.2 predicted that frd1, kgd1, sdh1, ald4, ald5 and ald6 mutants grow normally, that the frd1 osm1 double mutant does not grow (Arikawa et al. 1998; Camarasa et al. 2007), and that glycerol production is lost in the gpd1 gpd2 mutant (Hubmann et al. 2011); ethanol yields were predicted within 1% of the wild type, as measured (0.96-1.08). With the measured glucose uptake rate imposed, the wild type and the adh3 mutant of Bakker et al. (2000) were predicted within the experimental error for growth and glycerol, but this agreement results from the lower glucose uptake of the mutant, as the model predicts no effect of the deletion at equal uptake, also when gpd2 is deleted together with adh3 as in Grigaitis et al. (2023); for the mae1 mutant (Boles et al. 1998) the model predicts a 2.4% lower biomass yield. The model failed to predict the anaerobic growth defect of gpd2, osm1 and the ald6 ald4 ald5 triple mutant, the lower glycerol yield of the gpd1 and gpd2 single mutants (0.6-1.0 of the wild type, against 1.0 predicted) and the lack of anaerobic growth of the gpd1 gpd2 mutant, which grew 11% slower than the wild type. In the latter mutant the model reoxidises NADH through overflow of the threonine pathway with export of propanol, and through glycine and succinate formation when propanol export is closed; we have not tested whether regulation or enzyme capacity prevents this in the cell.
